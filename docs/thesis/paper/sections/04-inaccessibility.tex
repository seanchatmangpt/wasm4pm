We now prove that eight classes of adversarial mutation are inaccessible to
acceptance. The phrasing is deliberate: \emph{inaccessible to acceptance} does
not mean the attempt cannot be made --- the threat model
(Section~\ref{sec:threat-model}) grants the adversary arbitrary submissions and
even raw infrastructure access. It means the attempt cannot become
\emph{accepted enterprise state} without violating one of the named assumptions.
For each class we give the attack as it occurs in practice, the proof, and its
instantiation in the running P2P example.

\begin{proposition}[Unreceipted Raw Mutation]
\label{prop:raw}
A direct state mutation that does not produce a valid receipt is inaccessible
to acceptance.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Consider a raw write --- a SQL \texttt{UPDATE}, a direct object-store \texttt{PUT},
an edited configuration file. Submitted as a candidate $x$, it carries no
receipt satisfying Definition~\ref{def:valid-receipt}: no canonical OCEL logs
are embedded (substance fails), no challenge nonce was minted before execution
(challenge binding fails), so $\verifyreceipt(R) = 0$ and conjunct C2 fails.
By Definition~\ref{def:accept}, $\accept(x) = 0$; by
Corollary~\ref{cor:refuse}, $x$ is refused or residualized. The mutated row may
physically exist in storage, but by Lemma~\ref{lem:chain} authoritative state
is the receipt-chain derivation, against which the row is detectable
corruption, not state.
\end{proof}

\emph{P2P instance.} The payments employee runs the raw statement
\texttt{UPDATE invoice SET status='paid'} on row 4711. The row changes; the
chain does not. Any consumer that derives state per Lemma~\ref{lem:chain} ---
the payment execution service, the auditor, the reconciliation job --- sees
invoice \#4711 as \textsf{submitted}, flags the divergent row, and the write
itself becomes forensic evidence against its author.

\begin{proposition}[Process-State Skip]
\label{prop:skip}
A change attempting a transition not admitted from the current process state is
inaccessible to acceptance.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Let the declared law admit \textsf{Pay} only from \textsf{approved}, and let
the current state be $s = \textsf{submitted}$. For the candidate carrying
$e = \textsf{Pay}$, conjunct C6 evaluates $B(\textsf{submitted}, \textsf{Pay})
= 0$, so $\accept(x) = 0$ and, by Definition~\ref{def:mu},
$\mu(s, e) = s$. No combination of valid credentials, valid signature, and
fresh nonce compensates: the conjuncts are independent, and C6 fails regardless
of the others.
\end{proof}

\emph{P2P instance.} The classic fraud --- pay an invoice that was never
approved --- requires the \textsf{submitted} $\to$ \textsf{paid} jump. Under
the framework the jump is not a policy violation to be detected later; it is an
inadmissible transition that never acquires state authority, the direct analogue
of an unenabled transition in a workflow
net~\cite{van_der_aalst_wfnet_soundness_1997}.

\begin{proposition}[Object Substitution]
\label{prop:objsub}
A change that binds an event to different objects than those the authority
signed over is inaccessible to acceptance.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The signature is computed over
$h(a \| e \| O \| s \| R_{\mathrm{prior}} \| p \| v \| t \| n)$, where $O$ is
the canonical OCEL~2.0 event-to-object relation. Substituting $O' \neq O$
changes the message, so $\verifysig(\sigma, h(\cdots O' \cdots)) = 0$ (C1
fails). Re-signing over $O'$ requires the authority's secret key, i.e., an
EUF-CMA forgery against ML-DSA --- a named channel, not a free move.
Independently, C7 checks $O'$ against the object lifecycles in the chain: an
approval relating an invoice whose chain shows no submission fails
$\operatorname{ObjectsValid}$.
\end{proof}

\emph{P2P instance.} The approver reviews and approves invoice \#4711; the
attacker redirects the approval to invoice \#4712 (their own). In a
foreign-key world this is a one-column edit. Here the relation
$\{\textsf{Inv\#4711}, \textsf{PO\#982}\}$ is inside the signed message, so the
redirect invalidates the approval it tries to steal.

\begin{proposition}[Backdated Change]
\label{prop:backdate}
A change claiming an earlier effective time than its true chain position is
inaccessible to acceptance as valid history.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Time in the framework is process evidence, not a data field: the position of
$R_t$ in the chain is fixed by $h(R_{t-1})$ (Definition~\ref{def:valid-receipt},
ii). Inserting a change at past position $k < t$ requires either a second
preimage of $h(R_{k-1})$ (BLAKE3 assumption) or re-signing every downstream
receipt $R_k, \ldots, R_t$ (signature assumption, for every affected
authority). Merely editing the timestamp field $t'$ of an existing change
alters the signed message and fails C1.
\end{proof}

\emph{P2P instance.} ``This approval happened before the auditor's cutoff
date.'' The claim requires the approval receipt to sit before the cutoff
receipt in the chain. It does not, and no field edit can move it.

\begin{proposition}[Replay]
\label{prop:replay}
A previously accepted change cannot be accepted a second time.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The replayed candidate carries its original nonce $n$, already consumed, so
$\operatorname{Fresh}(n) = 0$ (C5 fails). Minting a fresh nonce does not help:
$n$ is inside the signed message, so the re-nonced candidate fails C1 without
the authority key. Moreover $\sigma$ binds $R_{\mathrm{prior}}$; once the chain
has advanced, the cited prior receipt no longer matches the chain head and C2's
chaining condition fails as well. Replay is thus triply blocked --- by
freshness, by signature binding, and by process position.
\end{proof}

\emph{P2P instance.} A captured, perfectly valid \textsf{Pay} message for
invoice \#4711 is resubmitted to pay it twice. The duplicate-payment fraud
that reconciliation teams chase quarterly is structurally inadmissible.

\begin{proposition}[Schema-Valid Fraud]
\label{prop:schema}
A change that is syntactically and schema-valid but process-invalid is
inaccessible to acceptance.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Schema validity establishes only that the candidate parses into
$\Omega_{\mathrm{change}}$ --- it is the precondition for evaluation, not a
conjunct of $\accept$. A well-formed payload still independently requires C1
(authority), C5 (freshness), C6 (process position), and C7 (object
consistency). The genre of attack in which correctly shaped data is injected
through a permissive write path is exactly the conjunction
``parses $\wedge$ $B(s,e)=0$,'' and fails C6.
\end{proof}

\emph{P2P instance.} \texttt{\{"status": "approved"\}} is a perfectly valid
JSON document, accepted by every schema validator. The question the framework
asks is not whether the document is well-formed but whether an
\textsf{Approve} event is admissible from the invoice's current state, emitted
by an actor whose policy grant covers it, with a verifier-sealed receipt. Shape
was never the issue.

\begin{proposition}[Rogue Administrator Mutation]
\label{prop:admin}
An administrator cannot alter enterprise state except by emitting admitted
changes.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Administrative privilege is represented in exactly one place: the policy
component C3, where $\operatorname{PolicyAllowed}(a, e, p)$ may grant actor $a$
privileged event types. Privilege therefore enlarges the set of events an
admin may \emph{propose}; it does not waive C1, C2, C5, C6, or C7. A direct
infrastructure write by an admin is Proposition~\ref{prop:raw}; an in-band
admin change skipping process states is Proposition~\ref{prop:skip}. The
admin's emergency-override path, if the enterprise wants one, must be a
declared event type with its own boundary entries --- making the override
itself receipted, attributed, and minable.
\end{proof}

\emph{P2P instance.} The administrator ``unblocks'' a stalled invoice by
forcing it to \textsf{approved}. Under the framework the only lawful version
of this act is an explicit \textsf{AdminOverride} event --- admitted from
defined states, four-eyes if policy says so, and permanently visible in the
chain. The governance shift is from \emph{admin as superuser} to \emph{admin
as privileged process participant}.

\begin{proposition}[AI-Agent Hallucinated Action]
\label{prop:agent}
An AI-agent action that does not carry the admitted-change witness is
inaccessible to acceptance.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
An agent action is a candidate change $x_{\mathrm{agent}}$ with the agent (or
its mandating principal) as actor $a$. Nothing in
Definition~\ref{def:accept} is relaxed for automation: the action requires a
signature under a key whose policy grant covers $e$ (C1, C3), a verifier-sealed
receipt (C2), freshness (C5), and process admissibility (C6, C7). A
hallucinated action --- one outside the agent's mandate or the process law ---
fails at least one conjunct and is residualized. The residual stream is itself
valuable: it is a labelled log of what the agent \emph{attempted}, which is
precisely the observability that agent-governance frameworks
call for~\cite{ethos_ai_agents_2024,saura_llm_security_policies}.
\end{proof}

\emph{P2P instance.} An agent tasked with ``cleaning up stale invoices''
attempts to cancel forty active ones. Each cancellation is a candidate
\textsf{Cancel} event; for the active invoices, $B(\textsf{approved},
\textsf{Cancel}) = 0$ under the declared law, so the batch residualizes
rather than executes. The agent's operator receives forty receipts of refusal
--- a far better failure mode than forty restored backups. This converts
agentic systems from open-ended mutation engines into receipt-bearing process
participants, the governance posture argued for
in~\cite{van_der_aalst_no_ai_without_pi_2024,nist_ai_rmf_maturity_2024}.

\medskip

Table~\ref{tab:inaccessibility} summarises the failing conjunct for each
class. Two structural observations follow from the table. First, no class
depends on detecting \emph{intent}: every block is a mechanical predicate
failure, which is why the guarantees survive insiders and automation. Second,
the classes fail on \emph{different} conjuncts --- the predicate is not one
wall but seven, and the attack-surface analysis of the next section is the
study of what it costs to breach each one.
